# Hook Alternates — "When the Company Becomes Your Personality"

Three drop-in replacements for the opening. Each replaces everything from the H1 down to just before `## The handover`. The existing opening (car scene → "a founder fusing with the company") remains **Hook 0 / control**.

---

## HOOK A — "Ten Minutes" (cold scene, present tense)
*Strategy: drop the reader inside the car in real time. Shortest sentences, highest tension. Best match for the current title.*

---

I have been sitting in this car for ten minutes.

The meeting was flawless. The numbers were up. I said the right things in the right order and gave every person in that room the certainty they needed from me. Outside the glass, people are walking past a car that looks empty.

My hands are still on the wheel. There is no reason to stay. I stay.

This is what it looks like when a founder starts to disappear. Not dramatically. Not in the news. Quietly — in a parked car, after performing perfectly, right up until the moment no one is watching.

---

*Use it if:* you want maximum cinematic pull and a read-ratio play. Pairs with the current title.
*Trade-off:* slightly slower to reach the thesis; needs the strong title to carry the click.

---

## HOOK B — "The Second Answer" (question device)
*Strategy: open on the dinner-party moment instead of the car. Broadest relatability — every founder has been asked this question.*

---

At a dinner last spring, someone asked what I do.

The answer arrived before the question even finished landing. Polished. Instant. Effortless. Ten years of practice compressed into one smooth sentence.

What unsettled me wasn't the answer. It was the silence that followed it — because I went looking for a second one, and there wasn't anything there.

Most founders never notice the merger taking place: the company slowly becoming the personality, one reasonable decision at a time. There's no announcement. No signature. You just look up one night at a dinner table and realize the answer to "who are you" has only one line, and it's the company's.

---

*Use it if:* you want the broadest "that's me" recognition in the first 5 seconds. Works beautifully with **Title Option 2** ("I Built the Company. Then It Built Me.") or Option 4.
*Trade-off:* less visceral than the car scene; more conversational.

---

## HOOK C — "The Confession" (bold first line, thesis-forward)
*Strategy: highest-risk, highest-reward. A blunt first sentence that dares the reader to keep going.*

---

The company didn't take my personality. I handed it over.

One reasonable decision at a time: the hours first, then the calendar, then the moods, then — somewhere past the point anyone would call reasonable — the question of who I was. Nobody signs that merger. There's no ceremony. There's just a founder who is now certain, useful, and essential at all times, and no longer entirely present anywhere.

I know because I was one. Maybe you are too. Here's the test: someone asks what you do, and the answer is instant — but it's the only one you have.

---

*Use it if:* you want comment-section energy and a manifesto tone that tees up the manifesto piece ("You Don't Need a Coach…"). Pairs with **Title Option 3**.
*Trade-off:* least scene, most claim — slightly more "writerly," slightly less "lived."

---

## My recommendation
Publish with **Hook A** (control stays a close second). Reason: this essay's job in the sequence is *demonstration* — the reader must feel the disappearance before the analysis. Hook A buys you the most emotional credit for the six-step arc that follows. Keep Hook B in your pocket for a republished/expanded version or a newsletter edition.
